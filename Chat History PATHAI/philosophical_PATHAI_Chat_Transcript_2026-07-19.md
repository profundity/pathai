Sharp catch — and no, the second part doesn't automatically follow from the first. Let me be precise about both things, because I was using "counterfactual" somewhat loosely.

**What "counterfactual" precisely means:** it's a claim about an outcome that was *not* actually observed — literally, "counter to the facts." The strict version: for this exact same cell, at this exact same moment, what would have happened under the condition that didn't actually occur? You can never observe that directly, because you can't rerun history on the identical cell twice, once with the gene removed and once without. This is sometimes called the fundamental problem of causal inference — the true counterfactual is always unobservable, by definition.

**So how do real experiments deal with that, if the true counterfactual can't be seen?** They approximate it, rather than observe it: instead of the same cells in two histories, scientists use *comparable* cells in two different, both-actually-observed conditions — a treated group and a control group that are similar enough to stand in for "what would have happened to the treated group if untreated." That's why "counterfactual" gets applied to this kind of experiment at all: it's not literally counterfactual (both groups are real, both outcomes are actually observed), it's a designed proxy for a counterfactual comparison. I was using the word a bit loosely by collapsing that distinction — worth being precise about, since you caught it.

**Now your actual question — does "remove it, effect happens" let you deduce "restore it, effect reverses"?** No, and this is the important part: **that's exactly why the rescue step has to be run as its own separate experiment, not assumed.** Restoration might fail to reverse the effect for real reasons that have nothing to do with the original causal claim being wrong:

- The cells may already be dead or irreversibly committed to dying by the time you restore the gene — you can't "un-trigger" apoptosis after the fact.
- The removal tool (siRNA, in this case) might have hit something else besides the intended gene ("off-target effect") — if the effect was actually caused by that off-target hit, restoring the intended gene wouldn't reverse anything.
- Compensatory changes that occurred during removal might persist even after the gene comes back.

So the two halves of a rescue experiment are logically independent, empirically separate tests — and that's precisely why, when restoration *does* reverse the effect (as in your AKT example, using mouse AKT1 resistant to the human-targeting siRNA), it's such strong evidence: it specifically rules out the off-target explanation, because an off-target effect wouldn't have been fixed by adding back the correct human/mouse ortholog gene. If reversal were automatically deducible from removal, doing the rescue would tell you nothing new — its evidentiary value comes precisely from the fact that it isn't guaranteed, and passed anyway.

Now the actual question, which is a good one — here's the conceptual cause of that missing-vs-inventing shift.

**Rule-based systems (REACH-style) are recognizers with a built-in "abstain by default."** They work by pattern-matching: does this sentence's grammatical structure match one of the known templates for "X activates Y"? That's why its failure mode is missing things: it just doesn't fire, and stays quiet.

**A transformer-based LLM has no structural equivalent of silence.** It's a generative model — at every single step, it must produce a probability distribution over what comes next and select something from it. There's no "abstain" state built into the mechanism itself. If you ask it "what's the timepoint mentioned in this sentence?" and no timepoint is actually stated, it still has to output *something* in that field — and because its training taught it that sentences describing experiments usually mention a timepoint, the statistically likely completion is often a plausible-sounding timepoint, even though nothing in front of it actually said one. That's not a bug in the attention mechanism specifically — attention is just a way of weighing which words matter to which other words. The cause sits one level up, in the fact that the whole system is trained to always produce fluent, plausible continuations, not to produce "nothing" when evidence is absent.

**Can it be taught to abstain?** Yes, partially — you can train or prompt it to output "unknown" or "null" when nothing is stated, and that's exactly what your project's "grounding" checks are compensating for. But that's a *trained/prompted behavior layered on top of a system that must always emit something*, not a structural guarantee the way "no rule fired" is structural in a rule-based system. "Saying I don't know" is just one more plausible-sounding completion competing against others — it can lose that competition, especially when the training data strongly suggests a value usually exists here.

**Why this matters concretely for your project:** it's exactly why grounding had to be added as a separate, external, code-level check — literally testing "does this field's value appear verbatim in the quoted evidence?" — rather than trusting the model's own sense of when to abstain. You can't fix an architecture that must-always-answer by asking it more politely to sometimes not answer; you have to catch and reject the invented answers afterward with a non-generative check. That's the real reason the pipeline ended up needing both an LLM and a rule-based layer bolted together — the two failure modes (missing vs. inventing) come from two structurally different kinds of systems, and neither alone covers the other's blind spot.
