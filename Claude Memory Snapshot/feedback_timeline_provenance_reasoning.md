Validated example (PATHAI, AKT inhibitors): AKT1 was discovered and characterized first, treated as the prototype oncogenic AKT for years before AKT2/AKT3 were identified. Genetic studies later revealed paralog redundancy — killing melanoma cells requires knocking out all three isoforms together, not any one alone. Pharmacological AKT inhibitors were likely developed and justified against the earlier, AKT1-centric picture of the biology, before redundancy was understood — so their underperformance relative to genetic triple-knockdown may reflect an outdated founding rationale, not a flaw in the drug's chemistry.

**The general pattern:** when a tool, technique, or claim underperforms relative to strong-sounding biological reasoning, one thing worth checking is the *timeline* — when was the case for this approach established, versus when was a complicating detail about the target system (redundancy, isoform specificity, feedback loops) actually discovered? A tool can fail because its premise was never revisited after the science moved on, not because it's badly made.

**Why this needs calibration, explicitly requested by the user:** this is *one example* of a broader category — careful reading of scientific claims, informed by grammar and context (see the verb-attribution rule and the abstract-arc rule already in [[PATHAI's rubric]]) — not a universal explanation to reach for whenever something looks paradoxical. The user was explicit: remember that this kind of deduction applies elsewhere, but as one example, not a rule to impose on every circumstance.

**How to apply:** treat "check the rationale's timeline vs. the complicating discovery" as one candidate hypothesis among several (alongside simpler explanations like incomplete target engagement, dosing/toxicity limits, feedback reactivation, pharmacokinetics) — not the first or only explanation reached for. Only pursue it when there's an actual, checkable historical timeline to trace (a founding discovery, a later complicating finding) — don't manufacture a provenance narrative when a simpler mechanistic explanation fits the facts already in hand.
